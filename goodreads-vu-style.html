Goodreads has millions of users — and a shelving system that most of them have given up on. I ran end-to-end UX research to find out why, and redesigned the two features users complained about most.

💭   How might we make Goodreads' most-used features feel as effortless as the act of reading itself?

The Problem

Goodreads' two most-used features were quietly driving users away — not through bugs, but through friction so constant that people simply gave up and worked around them.

Shelving a book took too many steps to feel worth it. The book details page buried the information readers actually wanted behind a wall of scrolling. Neither failure was dramatic on its own — but together, they shaped how people felt about using the app at all.

The Solution

Two targeted redesigns — grounded entirely in research, not assumption — that reduce friction without asking Goodreads to abandon what already works.

A clearer, faster shelving system that finally separates reading status from personal lists. A book details page that gets readers to ratings, genre, or similar titles in one tap instead of a long scroll. Both stay inside Goodreads' existing visual language by design.

Research approach

I used 5 methods to avoid relying on any single source

Starting with a heuristic audit of the app itself, I then collected survey data, ran think-aloud interviews, and validated patterns across App Store reviews and online forums. Each method answered a different question.

Heuristic Evaluation

Audited the UI against Nielsen's 10 principles. Found violations in system visibility, consistency, and efficiency.

Survey — 20 users

Asked regular Goodreads users about most-used features, frustrations, and wishlist items.

Think-Aloud Interviews — 2

Observed users completing real tasks. Both hesitated on the shelving flow — unprompted.

App Reviews & Forums

Validated patterns across App Store, Reddit, and Goodreads forums. Shelving and book details were consistently the top complaints.

Affinity Mapping

Synthesized all findings in FigJam. Used an impact-effort matrix to prioritize which problems to solve.

Speaking to users

Users weren't confused — they had just given up

Across interviews and survey responses, the most common theme wasn't frustration — it was resignation. Users had found workarounds or simply stopped using features rather than fight through the friction.

Too many steps

"I never bother with custom shelves anymore. It's just too many taps to get there."

Conceptual confusion

"I never really knew if I was filing a book or just marking it."

Missing status

"I have like fifteen books in 'Currently Reading' I'm never going to finish. It's embarrassing."

Buried information

"I have to scroll forever just to find what genre the book even is."

No private space

"I want to write 'this hit different the second time' but I don't want to post it publicly."

Opportunities

Five problems, two redesigns

Using an impact-effort matrix, I mapped the five research findings to two main design opportunities — the areas where a targeted intervention would have the broadest positive effect.

📚

Clearer shelving system

Separate reading status from personal lists — two things users kept confusing for each other.

✅

Did Not Finish status

Give abandoned books a proper home — not a hacked workaround shelf labeled "DNF."

⚡

Fewer steps to organize

Consolidate the multi-modal shelving flow into a single screen with one clear action.

🗂️

Navigable book details

Replace one long scroll page with tab navigation — get to ratings or genre in one tap.

📓

Private reading diary

Give readers a dedicated space for personal notes, separate from the public review system.

Redesign 01

Revamped shelving system & reading status

The existing shelving system styled reading status and personal lists identically — as floating tags — with no visual hierarchy between them. Adding a book to a non-default shelf required navigating through multiple modal screens. The redesign separates these into two distinct systems and collapses the flow to a single screen.

After

Consistent interaction patterns

The redesigned feature follows the same navigation conventions as the rest of the app — pages slide in from the right, iconography matches the existing system. Users don't need to relearn behaviors for an updated feature. This directly fixes the heuristic violation of consistency and standards found in the initial audit.

Reading status in one tap

The dropdown arrow on the status button opens a compact menu including the new "Did Not Finish" status. The button's color updates immediately to confirm the selection. This collapses what was previously a multi-screen flow into a single interaction — directly addressing the top frustration from the survey.

All lists on one screen

Every list is now visible and selectable on a single screen. Users can add a book to multiple lists without navigating away, and can change status from the same page. A public/private toggle (lock icon) is also introduced. This replaces the multi-modal flow that users described as the reason they'd stopped organizing their libraries entirely.

Redesign 02

Book details — from one long scroll to four smart tabs

The book details page packed everything — ratings, plot summary, genres, reviews, and similar titles — into a single continuous scroll with no way to jump between sections. Ratings appeared before the plot summary, creating bias before readers even knew what the book was about. The redesign introduces four tabs based directly on the four sections users navigated to most.

Before
[ Before screenshot — replace with your image ]

One page, one scroll. Ratings before the plot summary. No space for personal notes. Users had to scroll past editorial reviews just to see the genre.

After
[ After screenshot — replace with your image ]

Four tabs: Book Details, User Ratings, Book Diary, Similar Books. Title + "Add to List" anchored at top. Ratings behind their own tab. Book Diary for private notes.

Swipe or tap between tabs

Both gestures work — swiping and tapping — to accommodate different user preferences. The active tab's icon turns black, giving a clear current-location indicator that the original page completely lacked. Jump directly to ratings or similar books in one tap, no scrolling required.

Learnings & impact

What these changes could mean for Goodreads users

⚡

Fewer steps to organize

Adding a book to a custom list now happens from one screen. The multi-modal flow that was causing users to abandon shelving is gone.

🧠

Reduced cognitive load

Separating status from lists removes the conceptual ambiguity that was confusing even experienced users of the app.

🔍

Information where you need it

Tab navigation means ratings, genres, and similar books are one tap away — not buried under three paragraphs of editorial copy.

📓

A space that's just yours

The Book Diary gives readers a dedicated place for private notes — something they'd been doing in third-party apps for years.

Reflection

What working within constraints taught me about design

Working solo meant there was no one to sanity-check ideas against. I found myself returning to the research data every time a decision felt uncertain — if I couldn't trace it back to a specific user finding, I reconsidered it. That habit turned out to be one of the most useful things I practiced on this project.

The constraint of staying within Goodreads' visual language was the other. I couldn't fix everything I found wrong with the app's aesthetics — and that actually made the work sharper. It forced me to focus on structure, flow, and hierarchy rather than reaching for a visual overhaul. What I'd do differently: usability test the prototypes before finalizing. The research grounded every decision, but feedback on the actual redesigned flows would have added a layer of confidence I couldn't manufacture on my own.
