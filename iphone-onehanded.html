Reachability — Redesigning iPhone's One-Handed Mode

Reachability is Apple's built-in accommodation for one-handed use: a swipe down the home bar temporarily drops the interface so a thumb can reach the top of a large screen. It's a clever fix, but a temporary one. Drawing on HCI research methods and the ergonomics literature on single-handed device interaction, I evaluated how well Reachability actually holds up against the way people hold and use their phones, then proposed a control system, built into the keyboard, that extends one-handed use into full-time cursor control, zoom, scroll, and text editing.

Context

Bigger iPhone screens improve the content experience, but they reintroduce old physical-interaction problems

Apple's own lineup, from the compact SE up to the six-plus-inch Pro Max, treats screen size as a spectrum rather than a solved question. Research from the Nielsen Norman Group has found that people gravitate toward larger screens for complex or important tasks: more content fits into a single screenful, and there's less anxiety about typos on tasks that matter. But that size comes at a cost. Even on Apple's regular and Max-sized phones, a thumb is regularly asked to cover more of the screen than it can comfortably reach, which is why Reachability isn't a rarely-used accessibility toggle. It's something most iPhone owners already reach for.

Who actually reaches for Reachability

Four groups depend on some version of one-handed use, for very different reasons.

✋

Users with smaller hands

Thumb reach is a fixed physical constraint that scales worse every time screen size increases.

♿

Users with upper-extremity impairments

A second hand may not be a reliable option at all, not just an inconvenient one.

🤹

Users multitasking with one hand occupied

Holding a coffee, a railing, or a child makes one-handed use situational rather than optional.

🔧

Users relying on accessibility features

Reachability is often a load-bearing part of how these users operate their phone at all, not an occasional convenience.

Two features that were never designed to work together

iOS actually ships two separate answers to one-handed use: Reachability, which temporarily drops the whole interface down, and a one-handed keyboard, which shifts the keys to one side. Neither one knows the other exists. A user typing with the shifted keyboard still has to fight the predictive-text bar, which doesn't move with it, and neither feature does anything for zooming, scrolling, or careful text selection.

A gap left open by a company known for closing them

Apple has one of the strongest accessibility reputations in the industry. That's exactly why it's worth noticing that one-handed use, arguably the most common physical constraint of all, still isn't treated as a full-time mode. That gap is the reason this project exists.

The Problem

Existing one-handed modes on iPhone behave more like temporary accommodations than full-time interaction systems.

Reachability has to be re-triggered every single time a thumb can't reach, and it borrows against the rest of the screen while it's active. The one-handed keyboard only solves half its own problem. The next section breaks down exactly where each of these falls short.

The Mission

Build a real, full-time control system into the one-handed keyboard, not another temporary accommodation to re-trigger.

A joystick-style controller maps cursor movement, zoom, scroll, and tap-and-hold onto a single thumb-reachable area, designed around thumb-zone ergonomics research rather than a shrunken screen.

Current shortfalls

Three specific ways today's one-handed modes fall short

Less usable screen, every time

Reachability solves a reach by dropping the interface down, which means the fix itself costs screen space at the exact moment a user needs to see more of it, not less.

Research

Grounding the redesign in ergonomics research and a competitive audit

Part 1 — Reviewing HCI and ergonomics literature on single-handed device interaction

According to the paper Understanding Single-Handed Mobile Device Interaction (2006) by Karlson, Bederson, and Contreras-Vidal, diagonal thumb movement toward the far corner of a touchscreen is the hardest motion for a right-handed thumb to make. That finding explains a real gap in the current iOS keyboard: when it shifts left or right for one-handed typing, the predictive-text bar above it doesn't move with it, so the exact suggestion a user wants can still sit in the hardest corner to reach.

Caption. Thumb-zone mapping across iPhone screen sizes. The iPhone 6s Plus zone shape became the reference for this interface, since it best represents the large-phone reachability problem.

According to Steven Hoober's 2013 study How Do Users Really Hold Mobile Devices? published on UXMatters, roughly 49% of users hold their phone one-handed, and another 36% use a cradled grip that still depends almost entirely on the thumb. Together, that's about 85% of real-world use that iOS's current one-handed tools were never built to support full-time.

Caption. [Placeholder: chart showing the roughly 49% one-handed / 36% cradled / 15% two-handed split of phone grips from Hoober's study.]

Part 2 — Auditing how major phone platforms support one-handed use

Three platforms already ship some version of a one-handed accommodation. None of them treat it as a full-time system.

Samsung scales the whole interface down

Samsung's Galaxy phones shrink the entire interface into a smaller window that the user can resize by dragging its corner. It works, but it also raises a question: why pay for a bigger screen if using it one-handed means shrinking it back down.

Pixel shifts the interface down

Google Pixel's reachability feature moves the interface toward the bottom of the screen so the upper half becomes reachable, then returns to full screen after one tap. It's the same core mechanic as iOS's own Reachability gesture, re-triggered on every reach.

iPhone shifts the interface down with Reachability

A swipe down the home bar drops iOS's interface toward the bottom of the screen so the top becomes reachable, then it snaps back to full screen once the tap lands. A user reaching for the same button twice in a row has to re-trigger it twice.

None of the three treats one-handed use as an always-on mode, and Reachability is the only one actually built for iOS, so any fix here has to work inside Apple's own interaction language rather than borrow someone else's. More importantly, none of the three touches cursor precision, zoom, or scroll: shrinking or shifting the screen gets a button within reach, but it does nothing for pinch-to-zoom, careful text selection, or scrolling one-handed.

HCI framing

Grounding the proposed solution in the four waves of Human-Computer Interaction

HCI research is often described in three broad waves, each shaped by a different discipline, plus a newer, still-forming interest in accessibility and inclusion. Checking this concept against more than one of them was a way to make sure a "solved" feature like Reachability actually holds up physically, socially, and ethically, not just from a single angle.

👆

Wave 1: Human factors and ergonomics

This is where the thumb-zone research lives. Every measurement in this project, from Hoober's natural, stretch, and hard zones to where the joystick sits on the keyboard, treats physical reach as a hard constraint, not a guess.

💬

Wave 2: Social computing

Out of scope for this phase, since the goal is controlling the interface rather than communicating through it. A full-time one-handed system would still make quick replies and reactions easier down the line.

🕹️

Wave 3: Everyday usability and culture

This is why the concept leans on a mental model people already carry around: the way a thumb operates a trackpad or a game controller, rather than teaching something unfamiliar.

♿

Wave 4: Accessibility and inclusion

The anchor of the whole project. For someone with an upper-extremity impairment, Reachability isn't a convenience, it's often the only way to operate the phone at all. That's why nothing in this concept removes an existing option, it only adds to it.

Why it matters

Why one-handed mode deserves more than a quick fix

✊

Accessibility as the starting point, not an add-on

Designing first for people with upper-extremity impairment tends to produce better solutions for everyone else too, the same way curb cuts built for wheelchairs ended up helping strollers and rolling luggage. Reachability's biggest flaw is that it was never built as a starting point.

📈

A real case for investment, not just a nice-to-have

With roughly 85% of users holding their phone one-handed or in a cradled grip (Hoober, 2013), solving this properly turns larger screens into an ergonomic advantage instead of a reachability tax.

🧭

Even a design leader in accessibility has room to grow

iPhone has one of the strongest accessibility reputations in tech, which makes it easy to assume the job is already done. Reachability shipping, and getting widely copied, doesn't mean it actually solves one-handed use. It means the bar was set low enough that few people have questioned it since.

Constraints

Constraints that shaped every decision

Adding one-handed capability risked packing the keyboard with enough new controls to overwhelm the exact users it was meant to help. Every decision below was weighed against that trade-off, and against staying inside Apple's own design language rather than introducing something unfamiliar.

⚠️

Designing to prevent cognitive overload

iOS already has a dense set of interactive features. Each new control had to remove more friction than it added, or it didn't make the cut.

📱

Staying within iOS's existing design language

The concept reuses Apple's existing Reachability gesture and visual style instead of introducing something new to learn, and stays scoped to iOS to keep six weeks realistic.

🧠

Leveraging users' existing mental models

Anyone who has used a game controller already understands how a joystick should behave, so that familiarity does most of the onboarding work for free.

Ideation

Ideating an additive solution to bridge the gap between Reachability and the one-handed keyboard

Before deciding on a final design, the guiding principle was to stay additive: whatever we proposed had to sit on top of the existing Reachability system, not replace it or ask a user to give anything up. With that constraint set, two control metaphors were prototyped and compared.

Direction 1 — a laptop-style trackpad

The first direction added a trackpad option to the keyboard: opened on demand, it gave a thumb a flat surface to drag across to move a cursor-like icon around the screen. It worked, but a trackpad is a mental model most people associate with a separate hand, not a single thumb on a phone, and gestures like click-and-hold, scrolling, or zooming on this surface would need a second finger that one-handed use rules out by definition.

Direction 2 — a game-controller joystick

The second direction works the same way, but replaces the trackpad with a joystick that maps naturally onto a single thumb's actual range of motion. Most people already carry a strong mental model for how a joystick behaves from a game controller, so operating one with a thumb felt familiar rather than new, and that familiarity is what shaped the final concept.

Final concept

A flexible control panel built into the one-handed keyboard

The final design keeps iOS's existing one-handed activation gesture, but changes what happens after it. Instead of Reachability's temporary screen shift, activating one-handed mode slides a control panel into place of the keyboard, built around the joystick from Direction 2 and expanded with cursor control, zoom, scroll, and tap-and-hold, so it stays in the natural thumb zone for an entire task, texting, browsing, or filling out a form, instead of one single reach.

Turning the control panel on and off

The panel opens with the same swipe-down gesture iOS already uses for Reachability, so there's nothing new to learn just to turn it on. A visible collapse control folds it back into the standard keyboard the moment a task calls for regular two-handed typing.

Caption. [Placeholder: gif showing the swipe-down gesture opening the control panel, and the collapse button folding it back into the standard keyboard.]

Cursor control, borrowed from a mouse, not a trackpad

A central joystick moves an on-screen cursor the same way a desktop mouse would, with click and hold-to-drag built into the same motion. It solves the exact case Reachability was built for, reaching the upper half of a large screen, but without shrinking the interface or re-triggering anything first.

Context menus that follow the keyboard, not the original tap

Long-pressing the central joystick brings up the same cut, copy, paste, and look-up bar users already expect, positioned above the keyboard on whichever side the one-handed layout is set to, left or right handed, instead of floating wherever the original tap happened to land. That's the same shifting problem the predictive-text bar has today, solved for good this time.

Caption. [Placeholder: iphone-layout-setting.jpg currently shows only the keyboard width changing; replace with a screenshot of the context-menu bar shifting to match the chosen one-handed layout side.]

Zoom, without a second finger

Pinch-to-zoom needs two fingers, which a single thumb can't provide. A switchable zoom toggle turns the cursor into a small rectangular magnifying glass that can be dragged across the screen to zoom into whatever it passes over.

A dedicated scroll control

The joystick's main job is moving the cursor precisely, so loading scrolling onto the same control risked overwhelming it and causing accidental scrolls mid-task. Scrolling was given its own vertical swipe control instead, keeping the joystick focused on one job.

Limitations

Where this concept still needs real-world evidence

This phase of the project was research and literature driven: establishing whether the problem was real, and whether a joystick-based control panel was a workable answer to the gap between Reachability and the one-handed keyboard, before investing in a built, testable prototype.

🔬

Concept validated by literature, not yet by users

Every design decision is defensible against published ergonomics research. The next test is whether it holds up against real hands, motor-impaired or not, whose grip, thumb reach, and patience may not match what the literature predicts.

🕹️

An assumed mental model

The joystick is well-supported by ergonomics research and console-controller familiarity, but people would be navigating with it, not typing, and a phone's far more varied everyday tasks could surface issues a game controller never had to handle.

📱

Scoped to a single device platform

Scoped specifically to iOS phones, not iPads or other form factors, to keep the project achievable in six weeks. Android's more fragmented ecosystem, and other device sizes, would each need their own dedicated study.

Recommendations

What's next for this study

Recommended next steps

01

Run one-on-one interviews with current Reachability and one-handed-mode users, hearing any additional specific frustrations and struggles with the existing features beyond what this research phase surfaced.

02

Usability-test the joystick prototype with a genuinely diverse group, including different hand sizes, motor abilities, varying prior experience with joysticks and game controllers, and a range of phone-usage needs.

03

Improve the design based on user feedback and new findings, and revisit the concept itself if testing doesn't support it. The goal is the best solution, not proving the joystick right.

A note on scope

Most of this project's six weeks went into research: validating the problem and the interaction concept behind it, the joystick metaphor, the thumb-zone mapping, and the case for treating one-handed mode as a full-time system rather than a one-time gesture. What exists today is a proof of concept, so the visuals, animations, and UI still need real design work.

Reflection

Designing without access to real users

On research-first design

Every claim in this project had to be earned from published research instead of from a conversation with a real user. That meant leaning harder on sources like Karlson and Hoober than I would on a project where I could interview someone directly, and it was a useful exercise in a distinction I now pay closer attention to: a decision that's well-supported by research still isn't the same as one that's been validated by the people who'll actually use it. Neither replaces the other, they answer different questions, and this project could only really answer one of them.

On staying additive

The constraint that mattered most in hindsight was staying additive: never asking Apple, or the user, to give up the existing Reachability system, only adding to it. That discipline came from noticing how narrow the margin for error is in accessibility work, where starting from a blank page often assumes the current system has nothing worth keeping. Holding to that constraint, even when a from-scratch design would have been simpler to think through, was the hardest and most useful part of the process.
